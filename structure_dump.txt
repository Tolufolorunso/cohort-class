[48] [Heading 1] 1. Executive Summary & Curriculum Philosophy
[49] [Heading 2] Program Mission & Vision
[50] [Normal] Vision: To alleviate poverty through quality education.

Mission: To make quality education accessible to every child within reach.

The Dzuels Foundation Digital Literacy & Computer Education Programme is built directly on these founding principles. By providing secondary school students (ages 12–18) in Ekiti State and beyond with a structured, international-standard education in computer science, we aim to bridge the digital divide. Assuming absolutely no prior technology experience, this multi-phase curriculum provides a clear path to technological self-sufficiency, logical problem-solving, and future-ready employment.
[51] [Heading 2] Core Foundation Values (D-R-N-I-C-E-R)
[52] [Normal] The curriculum is designed to not only teach technological skills but also to cultivate character and leadership. The program instills the Dzuels Foundation core values of Discipline, Responsibility, No-Excuse, Integrity, Compassion, Excellence, and Respect in every classroom activity, laboratory session, and project delivery.
[53] [List Bullet] Discipline (D): Dedication to structured study and consistent laboratory practice.
[54] [List Bullet] Responsibility (R): Taking ownership of one's learning and caring for shared laboratory hardware.
[55] [List Bullet] No-Excuse (N): Overcoming infrastructure challenges through persistence, problem-solving, and simulation-based tools.
[56] [List Bullet] Integrity (I): Upholding academic honesty, proper citation of research and AI tools, and collaboration ethics.
[57] [List Bullet] Compassion (C): Supporting classmates through peer mentoring and cooperative team learning.
[58] [List Bullet] Excellence (E): Striving for the highest quality in code structures, slide design, and project execution.
[59] [List Bullet] Respect (R): Demonstrating positive digital citizenship, online safety habits, and respectful communication.
[61] [Heading 2] Curriculum Philosophy & Pedagogical Framework
[62] [Normal] Our educational philosophy centers on active, constructive learning. We believe that technology literacy is not acquired by watching, but by doing. Thus, this curriculum is heavily project-based, dedicating more than 60% of lesson time to hands-on keyboard, mouse, or code manipulation.

We balance three primary dimensions of learning:
1. Conceptual Theory: Building computational thinking, vocabulary, and structural understanding.
2. Practical Application: Mastering physical coordination (posture, speed) and software tooling.
3. Creative Synthesis: Encouraging design, logical problem-solving, and original code through individual and group projects.

By grounding students first in absolute basics (Phase 1), moving to programming logic (Phase 2), and finalizing with specialized modern technologies like Web Design, Git, and AI prompting (Phase 3), we ensure a gradual, non-intimidating transition into digital mastery.
[64] [Heading 1] 2. Teaching Guide & Classroom Management
[65] [Normal] This teaching guide outlines classroom instructions, laboratory configurations, and pacing adjustments necessary to implement this curriculum effectively across diverse school structures.
[66] [Heading 2] Teacher Preparation Protocol
[67] [Normal] Effective computer science education begins before students enter the lab. Teachers must perform the following preparation routines before every lesson:
1. Hardware Checks: Ensure all computer workstations are powered, mouse devices are connected, and keyboards are clean.
2. Software Configuration: Confirm that necessary software (Thonny, Scratch, Browsers, Office) is installed and updated on all student computers.
3. File Distribution: Pre-load any required exercise files or templates on student desktop paths or cloud drives.
4. Teacher Demonstration setup: Ensure the projector or screen display is functional and readable from the back of the room.
5. Backup Plans: Have offline worksheets and physical activities ready in case of power outages or internet failures.
[68] [Heading 2] Classroom and Computer Lab Management
[69] [Normal] Classroom management in a computer lab requires unique strategies due to the physical distractions of screens:
1. The 'Screens Down' Rule: Establish a clear signal (e.g. ringing a bell or saying 'Screens Down') where students must close laptop lids or look away from monitors to focus on teacher instructions.
2. Monitored Workspaces: Walk around the room constantly. Do not remain at the teacher's desk. Position screens so they are visible from a central walking path if possible.
3. Collaborative Pairing: If computers are limited, pair students as 'Driver' (typing) and 'Navigator' (reviewing logic). Have them swap roles every 15 minutes.
4. Safe Environment: Post rules clearly regarding food, drinks, and cable handling. Ensure students pack cables neatly after class.
[70] [Heading 2] Lesson Pacing and Duration
[71] [Normal] Recommended Lesson Duration:
- Total Time: 45 Minutes per lesson.
- Introduction & Concept explanation: 10 Minutes.
- Teacher Demonstration: 5 Minutes.
- Student Hands-on Lab Activity: 25 Minutes.
- Clean-up & Wrap-up: 5 Minutes.
Double periods (90 Minutes) are recommended for complex coding tasks in Python, JavaScript, and Web Design to allow deeper focus.
[72] [Heading 2] Differentiated Instruction for Varying Speeds
[73] [Normal] Computer science classes often have a wide range of learning speeds. Manage this through differentiated instruction:
1. Extension Tasks: For fast learners, provide 'Stretch Goals' (e.g. add a high score screen in Scratch, add styling rules in CSS, write nested loops in Python).
2. Peer Mentoring: Assign advanced students who complete tasks early as 'Student Helpers' or peer tutors to support classmates. This solidifies their own understanding.
3. Simplified Tasks: For struggling learners, focus on core objectives only (e.g. modify existing code variables rather than writing a program from scratch).
4. Visual & Audio Aids: Provide printed, step-by-step screenshot sheets for students who struggle with verbal instructions.
[74] [Heading 2] Inclusive & Accessible Teaching Strategies
[75] [Normal] Ensure the computer lab is inclusive to all backgrounds and learning profiles:
1. Language Adaptation: Use simple, clear English and write key terminology on the board. Avoid overly technical jargon when starting.
2. Gender Balance: Actively encourage female students to lead programming and engineering tasks. Set balanced group structures.
3. Ergonomic Support: Provide mouse devices suitable for left-handed students. Adjust seat heights and monitor brightness to prevent strain.
4. Neurodiversity: Allow students who get overstimulated by lab noises (keyboard tapping, fans) to wear headphones during silent working blocks.
[77] [Heading 1] 3. Assessment Framework & Evaluation Systems
[78] [Normal] To measure skills growth accurately, the program utilizes a continuous, multi-dimensional assessment system. This balances baseline checks, over-the-shoulder feedback, structured tests, and portfolio milestones.
[79] [Heading 2] Diagnostic Assessment
[80] [Normal] Diagnostic Assessment (Baseline):
Conducted before starting a phase or module. This consists of simple surveys, interactive discussions, or high-level questionnaires to check baseline technology exposure. For complete beginners, it checks if they know how to identify a monitor, mouse, or if they have used mobile devices before.
[81] [Heading 2] Formative Assessment
[82] [Normal] Formative Assessment (Ongoing feedback):
Incorporated throughout every lesson. Teachers check progress through:
- 'Exit Tickets': Short, 2-question slips turned in at the end of class checking core concepts.
- Over-the-shoulder checks: Reviewing code structures and typing styles during practical exercises.
- Think-Pair-Share: Asking students to discuss a programming problem with their neighbor and report back.
[83] [Heading 2] Summative Assessment
[84] [Normal] Summative Assessment (Final grading):
Conducted at the end of each module and phase. This includes:
- Conceptual Written Exams: Checking vocabulary, short answer logic, and syntax analysis.
- Practical Performance Exams: Timed laboratory tasks where students build files, folders, or scripts under observation.
[85] [Heading 2] Project-Based Evaluation
[86] [Normal] Project-Based Assessment:
Evaluates the synthesis of multiple skills. Individual and Group projects require students to apply their cumulative knowledge to solve real-world problems. Grading is based on a standard rubric assessing functionality, structure, aesthetics, and code logic.
[87] [Heading 2] Peer & Self Evaluation
[88] [Normal] Peer & Self Assessment:
Encourages metacognition and collaboration. Students review each other's code structures during peer-review blocks, checking readability and commenting. They also complete self-evaluation forms to reflect on their learning progress and identify personal gaps.
[89] [Heading 2] Cumulative Portfolio Assessment
[90] [Normal] Portfolio Assessment:
The ultimate evaluation tool. Students assemble all their completed works (Word files, Excel spreadsheets, Scratch links, Python scripts, and responsive website code) into a single folder or a remote Git repository. The portfolio showcases their growth from absolute beginners to functional software developers.
[92] [Heading 1] 4. Phase 1: BASIC (Foundational Literacy)
[93] [Heading 2] Introduction & Context
[94] [Normal] Welcome to Phase 1 (BASIC) of the Digital Literacy & Computer Education Programme. This phase is designed specifically for students aged 12 to 18 who have absolutely no prior experience with technology. The curriculum assumes that students have never touched a computer, seen a desktop interface, or held a mouse. The pedagogical focus of this phase is to demystify the computer, build confidence, develop physical coordination (such as typing posture and mouse grip), and introduce fundamental digital tools that are essential for modern education and future careers.

By the end of this phase, students will transition from absolute beginners to confident users of basic computer systems, operating systems, file management concepts, internet safety, and Microsoft Office tools.
[95] [Heading 2] Phase Description
[96] [Normal] Phase 1 serves as the critical foundation of the entire curriculum. It spans 16 weeks, comprising 32 lessons of 45 minutes each. The content begins with conceptual understanding (What is a computer? Where is it used?) and gradually moves to physical interactions (parts of a computer, keyboard layouts, mouse movements). As students gain confidence, they are introduced to software applications, starting with Operating Systems (Windows/Linux/macOS), file organization, safe web browsing, and introductory document creation and data processing using Microsoft Office. Every lesson balances simple explanations with hands-on practical exercises to reinforce learning.

[167] [Heading 2] Grading Rubrics
[169] [Heading 2] End-of-Phase Examination Framework
[170] [Normal] Phase 1 Final Examination Structure:
Part A: Theory (30 Marks)
- 20 Multiple Choice Questions (definitions, hardware parts, operating systems, internet safety)
- 5 Matching Questions (keyboard shortcuts to actions)
- 5 Short Answer Questions (e.g., Explain the difference between copying and moving a file)

Part B: Practical (70 Marks)
- Task 1: Boot computer, create a folder named '[Student Name]_Final', and create two subfolders: 'Text' and 'Data'. (15 Marks)
- Task 2: Open Word, type a paragraph of 5 sentences about internet safety, bold the title, format font to Calibri 12pt, insert a 2x3 table listing 3 safety rules, and save as 'rules.docx' in the 'Text' subfolder. (25 Marks)
- Task 3: Open Excel, create a table with columns: 'Item Name', 'Quantity', 'Unit Price', 'Total Price'. Enter 3 items, calculate Total Price using formulas, calculate Grand Total using SUM, and save as 'expenses.xlsx' in the 'Data' subfolder. (20 Marks)
- Task 4: Safely copy the entire parent folder to a USB drive or upload to a Google Drive path. Close all windows, log out, and safely shut down the computer. (10 Marks)
[171] [Heading 2] Certification Requirements
[172] [Normal] Certification of Basic Digital Literacy:
To be awarded the Certificate of Basic Digital Literacy, a student must meet the following criteria:
1. Minimum attendance of 85% in all Phase 1 classroom and laboratory sessions.
2. Submission of the Individual Project ('My Digital Profile') and the Group Project ('Computer Lab Guide') with a passing grade (50% or above on the rubric).
3. Achieve a minimum score of 50% on the End-of-Phase Examination (Written and Practical combined).
[174] [Heading 2] Module 1: Introduction to Computers

[176] [Normal] This module sets the stage for the entire curriculum by introducing students to the computer. Students will learn what a computer is, trace its history in simple terms, discover the different types of computers around them, and explore how computers impact modern society. The primary goal is to remove any fear of computers and build interest.

[194] [List Bullet] A short history: Early counting (abacus, Napier's bones, Pascaline, Analytical Engine) to modern microchips.
[195] [List Bullet] Types of computers: Personal Computers (Desktops & Laptops), Mobile Devices (Smartphones, Tablets), Supercomputers.
[196] [List Bullet] Where computers are used: Schools, hospitals, banks, supermarkets, homes.
[197] [List Bullet] Importance of computer literacy: Communication, career advancement, research, problem solving.
[199] [Heading 3] Classroom & Hands-on Activities
[200] [Normal] Practical Hands-on Activities:
[201] [List Bullet] Interactive demonstration: Teacher turns on a computer, inputs numbers via keyboard, showing how the computer calculates and displays them.
[202] [List Bullet] Computer Classification: Students look at pictures of devices and classify them (Desktop, Laptop, Smartphone, Tablet).
[203] [List Bullet] Field trip/walk: A walk around the school or local offices to observe where computers are placed and how they are used.
[205] [Normal] Student Activities:

[1484] [Heading 2] Appendix C: Computer Laboratory Rules Poster
[1485] [List Bullet] 1. Food and Drinks: Absolutely no food or liquids are allowed near the computer workstations to prevent spills and hardware damage.
[1486] [List Bullet] 2. Hand Hygiene: Wash and dry hands before entering the lab to keep keyboards, mice, and touchscreens clean.
[1487] [List Bullet] 3. Equipment Handling: Handle all physical cables, mouse devices, keyboards, and boards gently. Never pull cords forcefully.
[1488] [List Bullet] 4. Startup/Shutdown: Always start and shut down computers using the operating system menu commands, never the physical power button.
[1489] [List Bullet] 5. Assigned Workstations: Work only at your assigned computer workstation. Do not change seats without permission.
[1490] [List Bullet] 6. Safe Storage: Save all your work in your designated student folder. Do not modify or delete files belonging to other students.
[1491] [List Bullet] 7. Authorized Software: Do not download, install, or run unauthorized applications, games, or browser extensions in the lab.
[1492] [List Bullet] 8. Lab Cleanliness: Pack away all jumper wires, components, and pens. Push your chair in and shut down your system before leaving.
[1494] [Heading 2] Appendix D: Internet Safety & Cybersecurity Rules
[1495] [List Bullet] 1. Privacy Protection: Never share personal details online (full name, address, phone number, school, password pins).
[1496] [List Bullet] 2. Strong Passwords: Create passwords with at least 8 characters, combining uppercase/lowercase letters, numbers, and symbols.
[1497] [List Bullet] 3. Safe Downloads: Download files and software only from verified, trusted sources. Avoid cracked/pirated software domains.
[1498] [List Bullet] 4. Phishing Awareness: Do not click links or open attachments in emails from unknown senders. Verify sender addresses.
[1499] [List Bullet] 5. Secure Sites: Check for the lock icon and 'https://' in browser address bars before entering details on a website.
[1500] [List Bullet] 6. Cyberbullying: Treat others with kindness online. Report cyberbullying, threat messages, or mean comments to teachers.
[1501] [List Bullet] 7. Digital Footprint: Think before you post. Information posted online can remain permanent and affect your future career.
